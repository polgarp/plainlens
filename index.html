Plainlens design system workbench

Plainlens tells parents and designers what research says about children and technology. The design has two jobs: make uncertainty easy to see, and keep every verdict close to its evidence.

This page has the principles behind the design, then every token and component with why it is the way it is. The look comes from Isotype charts: thin exact lines, one marigold used like a highlighter, and shape before colour.

Principles 5

  1. Show how sure

    Certainty over confidence.

    Every verdict comes with how settled the evidence is, because that is the thing parents can’t get from headlines. Mixed and not studied are answers too, so they get their own marks.

    Used by verdict glyphs, certainty rail, verdict header, in practice, on age, evidence row and list item.

  2. Let shape carry meaning

    Shape over colour.

    Verdicts, support and basis are told apart by shape, so they read in black and white, for colour-blind readers, in dark mode and when printed.

    Used by colour, lines and shape, verdict glyphs, support marks, count figures, filter options and basis badge.

  3. Highlight, don’t decorate

    One accent over a palette.

    Marigold only marks what to look at: the clause a finding rests on, a selected filter, where you are. Kept rare, it keeps meaning something.

    Used by colour, chips, in practice, quote, guideline illustration and contents.

  4. Keep the evidence close

    Sources over summaries.

    Every verdict leads to a finding, a quote and a source in one or two steps, so you can check it instead of trusting me.

    Used by quote, evidence row, source card, basis badge and study row.

  5. Reuse before adding

    Fewer parts over special cases.

    The same rail, glyphs and heads appear on every page, so learning one page teaches the rest. A new part has to earn its place.

    Used by type, space, grid, support marks, certainty rail, buttons, link button, evidence row, contents, section head and group head.

Tokens 5

Colour

Everything is ink on paper except marigold, which marks what to look at. On the light ground it is only ever a fill under ink, since yellow text can’t be read there. Contrast is measured live in both themes.

Type

Jost brings the Isotype look to headlines. Atkinson Hyperlegible Next does the reading, since a lot of people read this on a phone, tired. Sizes are in rem, so the page follows the reader’s own text size.

Space

One scale of eight steps. Small steps keep related things together and big steps separate sections, so the spacing shows the structure before you read anything.

Grid

Every page splits the same way: 2 columns to find your way, 6 to read. You always know where the navigation is. Phones get one column.

Atoms 8

Verdict glyphs

A full circle holds, a level bar doesn’t, half is mixed, a dashed ring is not studied yet. They read at 16px and without colour. Side findings get plain arrows, so they can’t be mistaken for a verdict.

Support marks

Same circle family as the verdicts, drawn smaller, because both answer the same question: how much does the evidence back this?

Certainty rail

Where the mark sits shows how settled the evidence is. Mixed and not studied have no position, because there is nothing to place. One component, two sizes.

Count figures

Some shares are easier to see than to read. The figure always comes with its sentence, so it never stands alone.

About 1 in 6 apps played by 3–5 year olds used time pressure to keep them playing.

Parts 7

Verdict header

The answer people came for: the verdict in words first, then where it sits on the rail.

In practice

The choices a question leaves you with, each tagged by how well the evidence supports it. It isn’t advice. The tint sets it apart without adding another box.

On age

Always there, because “at what age?” is the first follow-up question, even when the research doesn’t split by age.

On age

No age cut-off shows up in the research. Most studies cover ages 12–16.

Quote

Word for word from the paper, with the place it came from. The highlight shows which part the finding rests on.

Evidence row

The verdict in small: its own claim, rail, finding and quote. Rows are grouped by what the claim affects, what changes it and why it happens, so nuance stays tied to evidence.

To write

Marks a sentence I still have to write. It shows on drafts and stops a page from publishing.

Source card

What the study is, what only it adds, and its limits, so you can judge it without opening the paper.

Patterns 11

List item

The question in the reader’s words leads, then the claim it tests and the verdict.

Empty state

When filters match nothing, show the closest questions instead of a dead end.

No question covers all of that yet

These miss by one filter. They may still help you make an educated guess.

Filter options

A round marker means pick one, a square means pick several, like radio buttons and checkboxes.

Squares, so what a guideline rests on never looks like a verdict.
